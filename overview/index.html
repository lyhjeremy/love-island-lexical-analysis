Love Island USA, season 4, 38 episodes

About 1,250 word families cover 95% of what anyone says on the show.

I scraped the subtitle transcripts for all 38 episodes, 224,815 words, and measured how much English a viewer needs to follow them. Published estimates put 95% coverage of everyday spoken English at about 3,000 word families. This show needs well under half that.

The question

How many words before you can follow it?

Intermediate learners are routinely told to watch native-speaker television, and most of it is too hard for them. A corpus turns "is this show hard?" into a number: rank every word family by frequency, add up their share of the speech, and read off how many families it takes to reach 95% coverage.

The comparison figures come from the published literature: about 3,000 word families for 95% of everyday spoken English (Adolphs and Schmitt 2003; Nation 2006), and about the same for TV dialogue in general (Webb and Rodgers 2009). This show reaches 95% at 1,249 families, 90% at 592 and 98% at 2,554.

Reading level

Grade 2.8 by Flesch-Kincaid, about grade 7 by the stricter formulas

The formulas disagree by four grades. Flesch-Kincaid, which leans on sentence length, scores the short spoken sentences as very easy, and every episode falls between 2.3 and 3.7. SMOG and Dale-Chall weight long and unfamiliar words more, and read higher. Only 2.1% of the words spoken fall outside the Dale-Chall list of 3,000 familiar words.

The distribution

Thirty words are 40% of everything said

Word frequency follows Zipf's law closely: regressing log frequency on log rank gives R² = 0.981 and a slope of −1.45, as in most natural language. What stands out is how much the head carries. The top 10 words are 22.7% of all speech, the top 30 are 39.8%, the top 100 are 61.0% and the top 1,000 are 91.1%. "Like" alone is 2.8%.

    Across the season

    No episode-to-episode trend in variety or tone

    Lexical diversity averages an MTLD of 59.96 per episode with no trend across the season (Spearman ρ = −0.03, p = 0.87). Sentiment polarity from TextBlob is positive in every episode, also with no trend (ρ = −0.15, p = 0.37). Episode 38, the last, is the longest at 11,366 words.

    Texture

    Verbs and filler, few adjectives

    Adjectives are only 8.4% of words, against 24.5% verbs and 13.6% pronouns. The filler words are frequent enough to chart on their own: "like" appears 28 times per 1,000 words, "know" 13, "yeah" 12 and "just" 12. This is unscripted speech, and unscripted speakers lean on a small stock of words.

    Limitations

    One season, read from subtitles

    This is one season of one show, and generalizing to reality TV as a genre would take more seasons to compare against.

    Subtitles drop what makes speech hard to follow: overlapping talk, accents and speed of delivery. A show can be lexically easy and still hard to parse at full speed, so the coverage number is a lower bound on difficulty.

    Word families are approximated by WordNet lemmatization, which merges inflections ("making" to "make") but not derived forms. Readability formulas were built for written prose and disagree on this dialogue, from grade 3 to about 7.

    Method

    Where the numbers come from

    The repository ships the cleaned corpus, one .txt per episode plus a combined CSV, so every number can be checked. The paper is written in Markdown, converted to LaTeX by a script in the repo, and compiled to PDF.

    Corpus
    Subtitle transcripts of all 38 episodes of Love Island USA season 4 (2022), scraped with Selenium; 224,815 tokens, 7,856 word types
    Coverage
    Tokens lemmatized with WordNet to approximate word families, ranked by frequency, cumulative share read off at 80, 90, 95 and 98%
    Diversity
    TTR, root TTR and MTLD per episode; MTLD because it holds steady across episode lengths
    Readability
    textstat: Flesch-Kincaid, Flesch Reading Ease, SMOG, Dale-Chall
    Other
    NLTK part-of-speech tags, TextBlob sentiment, Pearson and Spearman tests for trends across the season
    Built with
    Python, pandas, NLTK, TextBlob, textstat, SciPy, matplotlib, seaborn; the charts on this page are hand-written SVG and HTML drawn from the CSVs in V4/tables/
